# Trends Journal, 10 November 2026: Statistical & Trend Report

Nov 10, 2026 · @crafty

## Executive summary

The 156-page issue argues that the 3 November U.S. midterms, which gave Democrats both chambers of Congress, set off the Journal's "Dot-Com Bust 2.0." In its account two forces drive it: a voter backlash against AI data centers and an energy shock from the Iran War. The issue's statistics fall into six patterns.

1. **The election triggered an AI equity crash.** About $3.2 trillion of AI-linked value disappeared within 48 hours. On 5 November the Nasdaq fell 5.4%, the Philadelphia Semiconductor Index 11.9%, and the "Magnificent Seven" about $1 trillion. For the week, Korea's KOSPI fell 10.25% and Japan's Nikkei 10.74%, the Nikkei's worst week since March 2020.
2. **The backlash against data centers became policy.** Voters passed 40 of 41 ballot measures in 10 states to restrict data centers, and about 75% of midterm voters (AP) do not want one in their community. More than 100 groups in 15 European countries staged "Stop Dirty Data Centers" protests four days later.
3. **Energy is driving inflation and rate hikes across economies.** U.S. CPI is about 4% year on year (+0.4% in October), Eurozone inflation is 4.0% with energy at +20.8%, and Australia's is 4.3% with its policy rate at an 18-year high of 4.85%. Markets now fully price a December Fed hike. The Journal calls this mix "Dragflation."
4. **The labor market is running at two speeds.** Payrolls beat forecasts (+165,000 against 59,000 expected; unemployment 4.1%). Long-term joblessness, however, hit 1.96 million, the most since December 2021. This week's trackers log 42 layoff entries (about 30,000 quantified jobs) and 65 bankruptcies or closures.
5. **The war moves money from consumers to producers.** Fifty-one U.S. oil and gas firms earned more than $130 billion in war-linked profit from April to September, against under $47 billion a year earlier. Households paid an extra $150 billion for fuel (about $1,150 each), and gasoline rose from $2.98 to $4.15 a gallon (+39%).
6. **Politics realigned around cost of living.** Voters aged 18-29 backed Democrats 66-31, a 35-point margin against 14 points in 2024. Three in four rated the economy poor, and Trump's approval stands at 33% against 61% disapproval. Elon Musk's spending of more than $470 million and AIPAC's of more than $55 million on losing candidates bought few wins.

**How to read the figures.** The issue mixes reported data, mostly attributed to the FT, AP, CNBC and Reuters, with strongly worded editorial forecasts (38 "TREND FORECAST" and 19 "TRENDPOST" blocks). This report gives figures as printed and separates them from the Journal's opinions. None has been independently verified, and Appendix H lists 14 internal inconsistencies.

## Pattern 1: The election shock and the AI equity crash

Within a day, markets went from euphoria to crash. The Dow gained 1,569 points on 4 November, its best day since April 2025, then fell 1,844 points on 5 November, the worst day since then for all three major indexes. The trigger, the issue says, was investors absorbing the 40 data-center restrictions passed out of 41 measures, on top of AI debt and rising rates.

The week's losses were deepest where memory-chip and AI exposure is highest. Chinese indexes rose as Beijing's state funds bought.

**Scale.** The issue puts the total loss at about $3.2 trillion of AI-linked market value in 48 hours. The Philadelphia Semiconductor Index fell 11.9% in one day and is 38% below its October high, deep in bear-market territory. Hedge fund Walleye closed part of its equities business.

**How the market commentators split.** Bulls (MAI Capital, Truist) call AI an industrial revolution and still "the dominant theme" of the bull market. Bears (Benzaiten Macro) expect any rally to fade. A middle view (Cresset) reads the vote as a judgment on data centers rather than on AI itself. Quilter's Stuart Clark notes the structural risk: a handful of tech and chip stocks now carry so much index weight that their swings move the whole market.

**Forward signals in the reporting.** Dan Ives expects Big Tech's AI capital spending to peak in 2027 rather than 2028-29. He names cloud providers, power suppliers and some chip and memory makers as the likely losers.

**Crypto moved with risk assets.** Bitcoin is down 16.20% year to date and about 7% since the election. The TRUMP, MELANIA and WLFI tokens all hit all-time lows (Gizmodo).

**Journal position.** The Journal says the Dot-Com Bust 2.0 has begun. The Journal recalls its October 1999 call that tech would crash by Q2 2000. It expects gold and silver to spike once equities break, and it is "no longer bullish" on Bitcoin.

## Pattern 2: Energy shock, inflation and rate hikes ("Dragflation")

All three economies the issue tracks have inflation at or above 4%, roughly double their central banks' targets, and in each case the energy shock from the Iran War is the named driver. Every one of their central banks is hiking or expected to hike.

| Economy | Latest inflation | Target | Policy stance (as reported) | Growth risk cited |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| United States | \~4% y/y in Oct (NYT analysis of BLS); +0.4% m/m after +0.6% in Sept | 2% | December hike priced in: odds of 25 bp "above 100%", implying some chance of 50 bp (Bloomberg). Gov. Waller floats 50 bp | Journal: Dragflation. Consumer sentiment 51.0 |
| Eurozone (21 countries) | 4.0% Oct (from 3.8%), highest since early 2023; core 2.5%; energy +20.8% | 2% | ECB hiked in June and September; ING sees December as "one step closer" | Lagarde: higher inflation, lower growth |
| Australia | 4.3% Oct | 2-3% | RBA 4.85%, an 18-year high after its 5th hike of 2026 (6-3 vote) | HSBC: 50-50 recession odds |

**The energy channel.** U.S. gasoline went from $2.98 a gallon before the war (27 February) to about $4.15 on Election Day (AAA). The IEA expects global LNG supply to fall a record 3% this year. It warns that Europe's gas storage could drop below 10% of capacity by March, and that supply stays tight into 2028. Brent nonetheless fell about 10.5% in election week to $98.32, then to $91.49 by 10 November, on hopes of a deal with Iran.

**Pass-through to households.** Over April to September, U.S. consumers spent an extra $150 billion on fuel (Brown University's Climate Solutions Lab). The World Bank forecasts commodity prices 23% higher this year. The FAO food price index reached its highest since 2022 in August and rose another 1.5% in September. Australian mortgage holders face up to AU$6,600 (about US$4,610) more a year.

**Who gains.** ExxonMobil earned $15.4B in Q3 and Chevron $9.9B. Valero's profit rose sixfold to $6.83B, and Phillips 66 went from $133M to a record $5.62B. In aggregate, 51 U.S. oil and gas firms earned more than $130B in war-linked profit, against under $47B in the same period of 2025 (NYT analysis).

**Fiscal backdrop.** The issue cites FiscalData for U.S. debt-to-GDP of about 125-127%, on federal debt of $40.17-40.57T against GDP of $31.87-32.24T. Gregory Mannarino's guest column reports the Treasury raising its liquidity-support buybacks from at most $38B to $70B a quarter, an 84% increase.

**Journal position.** Expect more Dragflation (falling growth with rising prices) in the U.S., the Eurozone and Australia. Energy prices keep rising while the Iran and Ukraine wars go on. Asia and Europe will bid against each other for gas through 2027.

&#91;embedded content: Trends Journal, 10 Nov 2026, Eurozone inflation article (p. 32-33), citing the EU statistics agency flash estimate and Anadolu\]

Core inflation has not moved yet, which is why ING expects December's ECB hike to be the last. Moody's expects fuel costs to spread and force another hike in March.

## Pattern 3: A two-speed labor market and rising business failures

Top-line U.S. job numbers improved even as signs of distress got worse. October payrolls rose by 165,000, nearly triple the forecast, but the average spell of unemployment lengthened to 27.1 weeks. That is past the standard 26 weeks of unemployment benefits.

**Layoff tracker (week 203).** The Journal lists 42 employers announcing cuts, 28 with a stated headcount, for about 30,000 jobs. Of these, 458 are only "possible." Five employers account for 73% of the quantified total: BT Group (8,000), KLM (5,000), Schaeffler (3,400), Fiserv (3,000) and National Australia Bank (2,500). By region, Europe has the most quantified jobs (16,773 from 5 employers), but the U.S. has the most entries (28). The full sector-by-region cross-tab is in Appendix C.

**Going out of business.** The issue logs 8 bankruptcies and 57 closures. Restaurants and food make up 23 of the 65, retail 11, and healthcare, education/childcare and manufacturing 7 each. The Northeast has the most entries where a state is given (21), with Florida and New York at 6 each. Healthcare failures (Holy Redeemer, Roseland Community Hospital, Tapestry Health, Sharon Regional) stand out as a category beyond retail.

**Journal position.** Job growth will stay constrained until energy supplies normalize after the Iran War. The Journal reads the manufacturing, transport and professional-services gains as largely an AI-buildout effect, which the BLS does not break out.

## Pattern 4: The backlash against AI and data centers

In the issue's account, AI lost the election 40 to 1. The 41 data-center measures on ballots in 10 states passed in every place but Harwood, N.D. (population about 850), which rejected stricter review 3 to 1. The backlash now reaches governors' offices, the incoming Congress and Europe.

**Where the pipeline is exposed.** About 45% of planned U.S. data centers sit in states where Democratic governors and legislatures will probably impose restrictions (Goldman Sachs). Some 41% are in states whose governors-elect have called for a pause (Clearview). Texas alone has about 800 centers operating, under construction or planned, and about 248 new ones, a quarter of all those on the drawing board. Atlanta has about 2.9 GW under construction and is overtaking northern Virginia as the largest hub (CBRE). Twenty-six gubernatorial candidates in 21 states called for some form of pause.

The pattern: states and towns lean to **bans and pauses**, while federal Democrats lean to **cost allocation and model oversight**. Trump's promised veto makes the federal route slower.

**Voter signal.** In a CNBC poll, 56% said AI and data centers were key to their vote, and 65% of that group voted Democratic. A majority in an October Reuters survey say the administration does not take AI risk seriously enough.

**Europe.** More than 100 groups in 15 countries (among them Denmark, Finland, France, Ireland, the Netherlands, Spain and the U.K.) protested on the weekend of 7-8 November, calling for moratoria. European data-center investment doubled from €12B in 2020 to as much as €26B this year (PwC).

**Credit and capital.** Apollo lent a record $114B to AI companies and projects in Q3, up more than 50% on Q2, and more than $350B over 12 months. Its insurance arm booked a $1B net loss on loan markdowns. Ares declines to lend because it cannot underwrite how fast AI chips depreciate (they are said to be obsolete in about three years).

**China's parallel bubble.** The CSI 300 fell 8.5% in October, its worst month in more than 10 years, and is down 15% for the year against a 10% gain for the S&P 500. On 3 November, state-directed ETFs took in a net $11.5B, the second-highest day on record. The STAR 50 rose 9.9% and ChiNext 6.4%. Regulators have also paused IPOs, which had raised about $44B so far this year, and large block sales.

**Journal position.** Some controls are now inevitable, and other AI labs will copy OpenAI's compact. Slower U.S. buildout hands China an advantage, and the Journal forecasts China becoming the global AI leader. Apollo will write down AI loans in Q4 and 2027.

## Pattern 5: The Iran War and the Israel fronts

The Iran War, which the U.S. and Israel launched on 28 February, is in its ninth month with no deal. In the issue's account it is the main cause of both the energy shock and the Republican defeat. On 1 March Trump predicted it would last four or five weeks.

**Cost to the U.S. (as reported).** At least 20 U.S. service members have been killed. The war cost at least $47B as of October, not counting damage to regional bases. Gasoline is up about 40% since it began (FT), and about six in 10 Election Day voters disagreed with the decision to go to war (AP).

**Regional exposure.** Between 28 February and 8 April, Iran launched more than 700 missiles and drones at Bahrain and more than 2,800 at the UAE (The Economist). Iran says 11 tankers were attacked in the past 10 days, and the Houthis struck Saudi Arabia's East-West pipeline on 9 November. Traffic through Hormuz was 70% below its prewar level as of 3 November (earlier issue).

**Why no deal: Iran's MOU conditions (MOU signed 17 June)**

1. End the U.S. naval blockade.
2. Release about $100B of frozen Iranian assets.
3. Lift the international economic and maritime sanctions.
4. Pay compensation for U.S. and Israeli bomb damage.
5. Recognize Iran's sovereignty over the Strait of Hormuz.
6. Israel withdraws from Lebanon.
7. Israel ends hostilities in Gaza and the West Bank ("end to war on all fronts").

Iran offers to reopen Hormuz within two days if the MOU is implemented. The Journal argues that condition 6 cannot be met because Israel will not leave southern Lebanon.

During the issue's week, daily reports logged at least 26 people killed by strikes in Gaza, plus 4 killed when an embankment collapsed. In 12 hours of rain, about 70,000 tents flooded (B'Tselem).

**U.S. military aid.** Trump says the roughly $4B a year will end by year-end. The WSJ counters with $7B already promised for the next two years and about $10B in Israeli orders. The 2019 MOU provides $3.8B a year, and total U.S. aid since 1948 exceeds $300B (CFR). Critics say aid is moving into a merged U.S.-Israeli defense industry (IMEU, the 2026 NDAA).

**Russia-Iran.** FT documents show Russia supplied helicopter-mounted missile launchers in May 2024, and the Telegraph reports talks on S-400 air defense. The two countries signed a 20-year cooperation pact in January 2025, without a mutual-defense clause.

**Journal position.** The Journal expects the Iran War to escalate before Democrats take office on 3 January, and does not expect a deal. It argues that U.S. pressure pushes Russia and Iran together, and that U.S.-Israeli military integration will raise aid under another name.

## Pattern 6: Political realignment around cost of living

The polls the issue cites point the same way: the economy decided the election, and the war, prices and conduct in office widened the margin. Democrats won by about 13 points nationally. Andrew Sullivan calls that the biggest repudiation of a party since 1974, and says only 1926 saw a wider margin.

**What voters said.** About 75% called the economy "not so good" or "poor" (WSJ) or "not good" (CBS). By 66% to 21%, voters said Trump's policies hurt the economy, with 10% saying they had no effect (CBS). Concern about democracy came second at 32%, and 90% said government corruption played a role in their vote (WSJ). After the election, Trump's job approval stood at 33% approve and 61% disapprove (List Wire).

**Where the swing was largest.** Young voters moved most. In counties that are 80% or more Hispanic, Democrats won by 40 points (FT). Only 9% of 18-29-year-olds told Harvard in October that the country was on the right track.

&#91;embedded content: Trends Journal, 10 Nov 2026 (p. 132, 136), citing Tufts CIRCLE via Axios; 2024 figures as reported by Axios\]

The Democratic margin among 18-29-year-olds widened from 14 to 35 points. Gains were similar for young men and young women.

**Money did not buy outcomes.** The 20 largest donors gave about $2B to federal races (NYT). Elon Musk spent more than $470M, about half of it on Ken Paxton's losing Texas Senate race, and a source told the FT he lost every race he showed up in. AIPAC spent more than $55M on candidates who lost, including about $32M in Michigan, where Abdul El-Sayed became the first Muslim U.S. senator. That compares with AIPAC's super PAC sending 87% of its money to winners in 2024. Appendix D has the donor-by-outcome table.

**Opinion on Israel has shifted.** Americans rate Israel 44 on a 0-100 scale, the lowest since 1978. For the first time, a majority (56%) want military aid cut or ended (Chicago Council). Across 36 countries, a median of 67% view Israel unfavorably and 25% favorably (Pew).

**The administration's response.** Trump plans to govern by executive order ("Mr. Veto"). He has named Stephen Miller White House chief of staff, signaled some flexibility on immigrants with no criminal record, and questioned the election's honesty on 9 November.

**Journal position.** The Journal's Top Trend 2026, the "Gen Z Revolution," has "taken hold" and will widen into a "Gen Z+" revolution. Trump will double down rather than adjust. Democrats will have limited power over foreign policy and may step up support for Ukraine.
